# Debate transcript: Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd v SwiftHaul Couriers Ltd

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] SwiftHaul is liable for breach of contract for the delayed delivery. The standard of care in such a contractual duty is objective and demands a reasonably competent carrier, which SwiftHaul failed by mis-delivering the parcel significantly late, breaching the contract terms (J11:p3; F4). — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4
- **A1.2** [I2] The lost ordinary profits of about £18,000 are recoverable because the loss arose naturally and was in the contemplation of the parties, as SwiftHaul knew the parcel was for immediate use in the bakery’s business. The exceptional wedding contract profits are not recoverable as they were unknown to SwiftHaul, consistent with Victoria Laundry (J35:p2; F5,F6). — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6
- **A1.3** [I3] Clause 9 may not exclude liability here because the bakery accepted the terms online by ticking the box, binding them to the terms per L’Estrange, and reasonable steps were taken to bring the clause to their attention (J40:p2; J41:p2; F3). However, this issue is adverse to the client, and we accept the clause binds the bakery but will argue its interpretation and fairness in later rounds. — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; *Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] SwiftHaul is not liable for breach of contract in respect of the delay as the standard of care must consider the probability and practicability of precautions; a small probability of loss of trading due to delay would not constitute breach (J12:p2; F4). — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4
- **C1.2** [I2] Lost profits are not recoverable because the special circumstances that the bakery could not trade without the component were not communicated to SwiftHaul, who could reasonably expect the component to be replaceable or spare (J34:p2; F1,F2). — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶2
- **C1.3** [I3] Clause 9 excluding liability for loss of profit was properly incorporated given the bakery accepted terms by ticking the box online, and the question is whether reasonable notice was given, not whether terms were read (J41:p2; F3). Hence, the clause is binding on the bakery. — *Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C1.4** [I3] As this is a commercial contract between parties of equal bargaining power who allocated risk by contract including insurance, a clearly worded exclusion clause such as clause 9 will be enforced according to its true construction (J44:p3; F3). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* SwiftHaul breached its contractual duty by delivering the parcel eleven days late due to a clear error in dispatch, falling below the objective standard of the reasonably competent carrier required (J11:p3; F4). The low probability of loss argued from cricket and flooding cases (J12:p2; J14:p3) does not diminish liability here, as the breach was a fundamental failure to perform the delivery service timely. — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4
- **A2.2** [I2] *(replies to C1.2)* While the exceptional wedding contract profits are not recoverable due to SwiftHaul's ignorance of that special circumstance, the ordinary lost profits of about £18,000 are recoverable since SwiftHaul knew the component was for immediate use in the bakery's business, akin to the boiler use in Victoria Laundry (J35:p2; F1,F2,F5,F6). The failure to communicate special circumstances distinguishes Hadley v Baxendale (J34:p2) which bars only losses from unnotified special circumstances. — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6
- **A2.3** [I3] *(replies to C1.3)* The bakery is bound by clause 9, having accepted the terms by ticking the box online, which is equivalent to signing a contractual document (J40:p2; F3), and reasonable steps were taken to incorporate the clause as it was presented at booking (J41:p2). However, the plain wording excluding all loss of profit must be construed strictly and in light of fairness given the bakery was not informed of this impact; reliance on broad commercial contracts enforcing exclusion clauses (J44:p3) should yield to proper interpretation rules in such consumer-like circumstances. — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; *Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416, ¶2; *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.4** [I3] *(replies to C1.4)* Though it is a commercial contract, the bakery could not reasonably have contemplated the scale of loss covered by clause 9 given the undisclosed importance of the component to its trade. Accordingly, the clause should be construed in light of the particular facts and does not automatically exclude liability for these losses (J44:p3; F3,F5). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* SwiftHaul is not liable for breach as the probability of harm from a delayed parcel is low and reasonable precautions were taken, so no breach arises; Bolton v Stone supports that foreseeability alone does not equate to breach when probability is small (J12:p2; F4). — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4
- **C2.2** [I2] *(replies to A2.2)* The lost profits of the bakery are not recoverable as the special circumstances—the bakery's entire inability to trade without the component—were not communicated to SwiftHaul, paralleling Hadley v Baxendale where losses were unrecoverable absent notice of special circumstances (J34:p2; F1,F2). — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶2
- **C2.3** [I3] *(replies to A2.3)* Clause 9 is validly incorporated by the bakery's ticking of the acceptance box online, binding them regardless of actual reading, per L'Estrange; reasonable notice was given as in Parker, making the clause enforceable (J40:p2; J41:p2; F3). — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; *Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C2.4** [I3] *(replies to A2.4)* As a commercial contract between parties of equal bargaining power, the exclusion clause should be enforced according to its clear terms; thus, it excludes liability for lost profits despite the bakery not informing SwiftHaul of the scale of loss, per Photo Production (J44:p3; F3,F5). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
