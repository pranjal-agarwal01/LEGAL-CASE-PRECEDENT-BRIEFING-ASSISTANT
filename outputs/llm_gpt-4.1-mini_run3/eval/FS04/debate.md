# Debate transcript: Rowe v Skyline Terraces Ltd

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] The contract between Mr Rowe and Skyline Terraces Ltd is frustrated due to the cancellation of the fireworks, which was the foundation and common purpose of the contract as evidenced by the advertisement and communications promising a prime viewing experience, similar to Krell v Henry where the cancellation of the coronation procession frustrated the contract despite the flat itself remaining usable. — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶1; *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4
- **A1.2** [I1] Frustration applies here because the supervening event destroyed the basis of the contract both parties regarded as essential, even though literal performance (access to the terrace) remained possible, following the principle in Krell v Henry that frustration arises where a state of affairs fundamental to the contract is destroyed. — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4
- **A1.3** [I1] The terrace's continued physical availability does not prevent frustration; similarly to Taylor v Caldwell, where destruction of the music hall excused performance, here the essential purpose of viewing the fireworks has been negated, excusing Mr Rowe's obligations to pay the balance and justifying return of the deposit. — *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶1; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶2; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶5

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The cancellation of the fireworks does not frustrate the contract since the terrace, bar, and staff remained available for Mr Rowe's use, making the event only less attractive rather than fundamentally different, in line with the reasoning in Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton where the cancellation of part of the event did not discharge the contract as other valuable performance remained possible. — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶1; *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶2; *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C1.2** [I1] *(replies to A1.2)* Unlike Krell v Henry where viewing the coronation procession was the sole basis of the contract, here the contractual terms only guaranteed exclusive use of the terrace with bar service and did not mention fireworks, so the cancellation affects only Mr Rowe's own commercial purpose and not the contract's foundation, as emphasised in Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton and Davis Contractors where mere disappointment or reduced profitability does not amount to frustration. — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶2; *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶2; *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶2; *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4
- **C1.3** [I1] *(replies to A1.3)* The destruction or unavailability of the physical subject matter, such as in Taylor v Caldwell, is key to frustration; here, the terrace was intact and accessible, so the contract is not discharged by frustration since the agreed performance was still possible, distinguishing this case from Taylor and supporting Skyline's claim for payment and retention of the deposit. — *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶2; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶5

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* The cancellation of the fireworks frustrated the contract because the advertised foundation and mutual basis of the contract was precisely the rooftop terrace as a prime viewing point for the fireworks, as shown by Skyline's advertisement and their promise of 'front-row seats' (F2), paralleling Krell v Henry where the cancelled procession frustrated the contract even though the flat remained accessible (J37:p1, J37:p2). — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶1; *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2
- **A2.2** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* Frustration occurs despite the terrace and bar remaining available, because the supervening event destroyed the fundamental basis of the contract that both parties recognized, consistent with Krell and distinct from Herne Bay where only one party's purpose failed and alternative performance remained substantial; here, the 'front-row seats' for fireworks was the core purpose, making performance radically different from what was undertaken (J37:p3, F2, F5). — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A2.3** [I1] *(replies to C1.2)* The omission of fireworks in the written terms (F3) does not exclude frustration, as the entire contract must be construed in light of the common assumption shared by both parties, including the promises in pre-contractual communications (F2), which established the essential purpose, like in Krell where letters and advertisements established the common purpose beyond the written terms (J37:p2). — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.4** [I1] *(replies to C1.3)* The fact that the terrace itself remained intact and usable does not prevent frustration, because frustration depends on the destruction of the contract's foundation rather than the physical existence of the subject matter, analogous to Taylor v Caldwell where destruction of the music hall discharged the parties notwithstanding the contract to hire a venue (J36:p2, J36:p3, F4, F5). — *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶2; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A2.5** [I1] *(replies to C1.2)* The defendant's argument that frustration requires the physical destruction of the venue and that Mr Rowe's sole purpose is commercial and thus risked the cancellation is rejected, because the parties' mutual assumption created a common foundation akin to Krell and Taylor, not a mere personal venture as in Herne Bay or Davis Contractors where hardship or frustration was denied (J37:p2, J36:p1, J38:p3, J39:p3, F2, F6). — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶2; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶1; *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶3; *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.2)* The cancellation of the fireworks does not frustrate the contract because the terrace, bar, and staff were still available, providing the agreed service, making the event merely less attractive rather than radically different, consistent with Herne Bay where seeing the naval review was only one party's purpose and performance of a cruise around the fleet was still possible (J38:p1, J38:p2, F5). — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶1; *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C2.2** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* Unlike Krell where viewing the procession was the foundation of the contract, here the written agreement only guaranteed exclusive use of the terrace and bar service without mention of fireworks; thus, the cancellation affects only Mr Rowe's personal purpose, aligning with Herne Bay and Davis Contractors where frustration was denied because the frustrating event rendered performance less profitable or more onerous but not radically different (J38:p2, J39:p2, J39:p3, F3, F6). — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶2; *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶2; *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C2.3** [I1] *(replies to A2.4)* Frustration requires the contractual obligation to become incapable of performance or radically different, not just more difficult or less enjoyable; here, performance was still possible as the terrace could be used, so this case differs critically from Taylor where the subject matter was destroyed, discharging the contract (J39:p3, J36:p2, J36:p3, F5). — *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶2; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C2.4** [I1] *(replies to A2.3)* The reliance on pre-contractual representations cannot override the clear contractual terms which focus on exclusive terrace use and bar service without fireworks; unlike Krell where the cancelled event was mutually the contract's foundation, here it was Mr Rowe's own commercial purpose, so frustration does not arise (J38:p2, F2, F3). — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C2.5** [I1] *(replies to A2.5)* To argue that frustration arises from the fireworks cancellation is to conflate inconvenience with impossibility; hardship or less attractiveness does not discharge contract obligations, a principle emphasised in Davis Contractors, unlike Krell which was dependent on an event central to the contract (J39:p3, F6). — *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
