# Debate transcript: Hughes v PeakFit Ltd

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] The PeakFit advertisement is a binding offer because, like in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, it contains clear terms and demonstrates an intention to be bound by a deposit with Barclays, showing it is not mere puff. Ms Hughes performed the conditions by logging workouts and weighing in, thereby accepting the offer by performance without need for prior notification, at least analogously to Carlill. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.2** [I2] Ms Hughes’ logging of all workouts and weighing in as required constitutes valid consideration; performance of the stipulated conditions in return for the promise constitutes consideration as held in Carlill. The activities were not pre-existing contractual duties owed to PeakFit, distinguishing this from Stilk v Myrick, because she was a customer accepting a unilateral offer, not an agent under contract performing duties already owed. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.3** [I3] PeakFit’s general term allowing withdrawal 'may be withdrawn at any time' does not preclude enforceability here, as the promotion was not withdrawn before the performance was complete on 15 April 2025 (F5). Under Photo Production Ltd v Securicor, explicit and clear contractual terms allocate risks, but the condition that allows withdrawal must be properly communicated and exercised before acceptance; here it was not exercised pre-performance, so does not absolve PeakFit. — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A1.4** [I4] The promise to pay £2,000 is not mere marketing puff. Like in Carlill, the deposit of £100,000 with Barclays evidences intention to create legal relations and seriousness. The clear, performance-based conditions imposed on participants demonstrate a serious offer rather than vague or mere opinion advertising, making the promise binding. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The PeakFit advertisement should be considered an invitation to treat rather than a binding offer because advertisements, particularly where many participants are involved and the promisor has limited resources, usually do not intend to be legally bound to everyone. This principle was upheld in Partridge v Crittenden, where an advertisement was held not to be an offer as the advertiser cannot be bound to sell to all responders, here more than 3,000 downloaded the app, making it impractical to treat the advertisement as an offer (J24:p2; J24:p3; F6). — *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C1.2** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* Similarly, in Fisher v Bell, the display of goods or advertisements with prices was held to be an invitation to treat rather than an offer to sell, reinforcing that advertisements are generally not contractual offers. The PeakFit advertisement should be regarded the same way, as PeakFit did not expect to be legally bound and clearly intended it as marketing (J50:p2; J50:p3; F6). — *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C1.3** [I2] *(replies to A1.2)* The consideration argument is weak because, by analogy to Stilk v Myrick, Ms Hughes' performance may amount to a pre-existing duty under the subscription terms or app usage, so no new consideration is provided for the £2,000 promise. As in Stilk, performance of existing obligations is not good consideration (J31:p3; F2; F3). — *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C1.4** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* PeakFit's general term allowing withdrawal 'may be withdrawn at any time' can operate to exclude or limit PeakFit's promise, provided it is properly incorporated and construed. Photo Production confirms that, in commercial contracts, clear allocation of risk clauses are given effect, so PeakFit's term allows withdrawal as a valid protection even if not explicitly exercised before 15 April (J44:p3; F5). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C1.5** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* Further, terms allowing withdrawal or exclusion must be brought to participants' attention before or at contract formation to be incorporated. Here, PeakFit may have failed to incorporate such terms clearly, but any ambiguity should favour PeakFit's interpretation restricting liability, per Olley v Marlborough Court and Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking, which require onerous terms to be clearly communicated (J42:p3; J43:p3; F5). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C1.6** [I4] *(replies to A1.4)* The £2,000 promise appears to be marketing puff rather than a binding contractual term, because PeakFit did not expect to be legally bound to all participants, and the advertisement was clearly promotional with many participants. This aligns with the reasoning in Partridge that advertisements cannot sensibly bind advertisers to all respondents (J24:p2; J24:p3; F6). — *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* The PeakFit advertisement is a binding offer because, like in Carlill, it contains clear terms and shows intention to be bound by depositing £100,000 with Barclays, making it more than mere puff. Ms Hughes accepted by performing the conditions stipulated, logging workouts and weighing in, without needing prior notification (J23:p2; J23:p3; F1, F3). This distinguishes it from Partridge, where adverts for goods with limited stock were held invitations to treat (J24:p2; J24:p3; F6). — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **A2.2** [I1] *(replies to C1.2)* Fisher v Bell applies to goods displayed for sale in shops, held invitations to treat, whereas the PeakFit advertisement is a unilateral offer to the world with clear performance conditions and a financial deposit backing the promise, making it more like Carlill than Fisher (J50:p2; J50:p3; J23:p3; F1, F3). — *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶3; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.3** [I2] *(replies to C1.3)* Ms Hughes' performance of logging workouts and weighing in was valid consideration because she undertook actions she was not contractually obliged to perform, distinguishing from Stilk where duty already existed. The unilateral offer in Carlill shows performing stipulated conditions can be good consideration (J23:p3; J31:p3; F2, F3). — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.4** [I3] *(replies to C1.4)* Though PeakFit's terms allow withdrawal, this was not exercised before 15 April when Ms Hughes completed the conditions. Under Photo Production, clear terms allocating risk are upheld, but here PeakFit did not withdraw the promotion in time, so the promise remains enforceable (J44:p3; F5). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A2.5** [I3] *(replies to C1.5)* Any failure to incorporate withdrawal terms clearly before contract formation favors enforceability of Ms Hughes' claim, per Olley and Thornton requiring clear notice before or at contract formation. Ambiguity in incorporation should be resolved against PeakFit (J42:p3; J43:p3; F5). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A2.6** [I4] *(replies to C1.6)* The £2,000 promise is a binding contractual term and not mere marketing puff, as evidenced by the deposit and clear condition performance required from participants, analogous to Carlill where these facts established an enforceable unilateral contract (J23:p2; J23:p3; F1, F6). — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* The PeakFit advertisement should be classified as an invitation to treat, not a binding offer, because it resembles a general advertisement to a large public and not a specific contract offer. The principle in Partridge that advertisements for sale are invitations and not offers applies here especially given more than 3,000 participants, making it impossible to bind PeakFit to all (J24:p2; F6). — *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C2.2** [I1] *(replies to A2.2)* The analogy to Fisher v Bell confirms that displays or advertisements are not offers but invitations to treat, undermining any claim that PeakFit's advertisement constituted a formal offer. The promotional nature and expectation of PeakFit that it was not legally bound supports this view (J50:p2; J50:p3; F6). — *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C2.3** [I2] *(replies to A2.3)* Ms Hughes' performance does not constitute valid consideration as she was already contractually bound by subscription and app usage conditions to log workouts and weigh in, mirroring Stilk v Myrick where no fresh consideration arises from performing an existing duty (J31:p3; F2; F3). — *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C2.4** [I3] *(replies to A2.4)* PeakFit's promotion withdrawal term can protect it since such clear, risk-allocating clauses are upheld where incorporated. Even if not exercised before 15 April, the right to withdraw exists, limiting enforceability of any promise, as per Photo Production which endorses strict construction (J44:p3; F5). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C2.5** [I3] *(replies to A2.5)* Any purported withdrawal clause must be effectively incorporated by bringing it to participants' attention before or at contract formation, which PeakFit may have failed to do. However, any ambiguity about incorporation should be resolved in light of authorities preventing onerous terms from sneaking in unnoticed (J42:p3; J43:p3; F5). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C2.6** [I4] *(replies to A2.6)* The £2,000 promise is promotional puff, not a binding contractual allegiance, because it is an offer to a mass audience without expectation of binding all participants, consistent with the reasoning in Partridge that advertisements are not offers to form multiple simultaneous contracts (J24:p2; F6). — *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
