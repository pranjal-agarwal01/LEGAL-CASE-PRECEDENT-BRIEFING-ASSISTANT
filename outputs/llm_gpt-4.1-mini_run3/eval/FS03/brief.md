# Brief: Hughes v PeakFit Ltd

**Case** FS03 · **Acting for** Claimant · **Prepared** 15 November 2026 · **Mode** llm:foundry

> Academic demonstration only. This is not legal advice. It was produced by a multi-agent prototype from a synthetic fact sheet and a small teaching corpus of paraphrased judgment summaries; verify any authority against the official law report.

## Summary

- Ms Hughes seeks to enforce a £2,000 promise by PeakFit made in an advertisement contingent on completing 100 workouts and losing 5 kg, backed by a £100,000 deposit. — Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- The claimant relies chiefly on Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, emphasizing clear terms, demonstrated intention to be bound by deposit, and performance as acceptance without notification, distinguishing the case from typical invitations to treat in Partridge and Fisher v Bell. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- While PeakFit's terms allow withdrawing promotions, the claimant argues this was not done before completion on 15 April, relying on principles restricting incorporation of onerous terms and emphasizing the timing of withdrawal. — Fact sheet ¶5; *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163, ¶3

## Issues

1. **I1.** Was the PeakFit advertisement a binding offer or merely an invitation to treat?
2. **I2.** Is there valid consideration for Ms Hughes' acceptance of the PeakFit advertisement's promise?
3. **I3.** Can Ms Hughes enforce the £2,000 promise despite PeakFit's general terms allowing withdrawal of promotions?
4. **I4.** Is the £2,000 promise a binding term of contract or merely marketing puff by PeakFit?

## Key facts

- PeakFit published an advertisement promising £2,000 to participants who completed 100 workouts and lost 5 kg by 15 April, stating £100,000 was held at Barclays as a sign of commitment. — Fact sheet ¶1
- Participants had to log every workout and weigh themselves on the app's connected scales on 15 April. — Fact sheet ¶2
- Ms Hughes downloaded the app on 6 January, paid £9.99, logged all workouts, and on 15 April weighed in reporting a 1.2 kg weight loss. — Fact sheet ¶3
- On 20 April, Ms Hughes claimed the £2,000 prize; PeakFit denied the claim stating the advertisement was marketing puff and an invitation to treat, not a contract, and that she should have notified them when starting. — Fact sheet ¶4
- PeakFit's terms allow withdrawal of promotions at any time but it did not withdraw this promotion before 15 April. — Fact sheet ¶5
- Over 3,000 people downloaded the app during the promotion; PeakFit claims it never intended to be legally bound and considered the advertisement as marketing puff. — Fact sheet ¶6
- Ms Hughes intends to enforce PeakFit's promise and claim the money. — Fact sheet ¶7

## I1. Was the PeakFit advertisement a binding offer or merely an invitation to treat?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 | An advertisement with clear terms and intentions, demonstrated by a financial deposit, can be a unilateral offer accepted by performance without notice. | *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204 | Advertisements are generally invitations to treat since advertisers cannot be bound to all potential responders, especially where stock or capacity is limited. | *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶3 |
| *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394 | Displays of goods or advertisements with prices are invitations to treat, not offers, preventing vendors from being bound to all responders. | *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- The PeakFit advertisement is a binding offer as it includes clear, performance-based conditions and shows intention through a £100,000 deposit, analogous to Carlill where such evidence established contract formation. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- Ms Hughes accepted the offer by completing the required workouts and weight measurements with no need to notify, consistent with the unilateral contract mechanism in Carlill, distinguishing from typical adverts held to be invitations to treat. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3

**Argument for the other side**

- The advertisement is an invitation to treat as per Partridge, because more than 3,000 participants and PeakFit's limited resources make it impractical and unintended to bind the advertiser to all. — *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- Fisher v Bell confirms that displays and advertisements with prices are not offers but invitations to treat, reinforcing that PeakFit's advertisement should be similarly classified as promotional and non-binding. — *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6

**Assessment**

- The claimant's reliance on Carlill is strong given the clarity of terms, evidence of seriousness by deposit, and unilateral contract framework allowing acceptance by performance without notification. The mass participation concern in Partridge has less force where the ad promises payment contingent on performance with a deposited fund backing seriousness, aligning more with Carlill than general invitations to treat. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6

## I2. Is there valid consideration for Ms Hughes' acceptance of the PeakFit advertisement's promise?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 | Performance of the stipulated conditions in a unilateral offer constitutes valid consideration even if no prior notification of acceptance is given. | *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317 | Performance of existing contractual duties owed to the promisor is not good consideration for a new promise. | *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- Ms Hughes' logging and weighing were fresh acts undertaken to meet the unilateral offer's requirements, and not pre-existing duties owed to PeakFit, thus constituting good consideration, drawing on Carlill illustrating performance as valid consideration. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

**Argument for the other side**

- Performance of obligations connected to app subscription and usage might be seen as pre-existing duties, analogously to Stilk v Myrick, negating fresh consideration for PeakFit's promise. — *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

**Assessment**

- Ms Hughes likely provided valid consideration because her actions were undertaken specifically to fulfill the advertisement's unilateral offer conditions and were not already contractual duties under subscription terms. This aligns with Carlill's approach and distinguishes from Stilk, which involved existing contractual duties owed to the promisor. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

## I3. Can Ms Hughes enforce the £2,000 promise despite PeakFit's general terms allowing withdrawal of promotions?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827 | Clear risk-allocation clauses are enforceable, but they must be properly incorporated and exercised; absence of exercised withdrawal before performance completion suggests liability persists. | *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532 | Exclusion or withdrawal clauses must be brought to the party's attention before or at contract formation to be incorporated; post-contract notices are ineffective. | *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3 |
| *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163 | Onerous exclusion terms require especially clear notice at or before contract formation to be effective. | *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- PeakFit's withdrawal clause was not exercised before Ms Hughes completed performance and thus cannot negate the binding promise; this is supported by Photo Production's emphasis on clear risk allocation but also by the timing and exercise of the clause. — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

**Argument for the other side**

- The withdrawal term could exclude PeakFit's liability if incorporated; however, incorporation requires clear notice before or at contract formation, and ambiguous or late notice should be construed against PeakFit, consistent with Olley and Thornton. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

**Assessment**

- Although PeakFit had a term allowing withdrawal, it was not effectively exercised before Ms Hughes completed performance. Moreover, any unclear incorporation of the term must be construed against PeakFit, favoring enforceability of the promise. This supports Ms Hughes' claim that the promise is binding despite the general terms. — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

## I4. Is the £2,000 promise a binding term of contract or merely marketing puff by PeakFit?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 | A deposit evidencing seriousness, combined with clear terms requiring performance by participants, shows intention to create legal relations and negates the puff argument. | *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204 | Advertisements made to mass audiences are usually regarded as promotional puff and invitations to treat, not binding contractual commitments. | *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- The £2,000 promise backed by a £100,000 deposit and clear fulfillment conditions indicates genuine commercial intent to contract, analogous to Carlill, opposing the characterization as mere puff. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶6

**Argument for the other side**

- Given the number of participants and PeakFit's own admission of the promotion as marketing, the promise appears to be puff and not a binding commitment, consistent with Partridge's view on advertisements. — *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6

**Assessment**

- The clear conditions and deposited fund favor treating the promise as a serious contractual offer rather than puff, consistent with Carlill. The opposition's reliance on general principles about advertisements as invitations to treat is outweighed by facts evidencing PeakFit's intention to be bound. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶6

## Conclusion

- The PeakFit advertisement constituted a valid offer, accepted by Ms Hughes through full performance of the stipulated conditions under a unilateral contract framework as elucidated in Carlill. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- Ms Hughes' performance provides valid consideration, not merely performance of pre-existing duties, supporting enforceability of PeakFit's promise. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- PeakFit's general terms permitting withdrawal do not apply as the promotion was not withdrawn prior to completion of conditions, and incorporation of such terms lacks sufficient clarity to negate the promise. — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- The £2,000 promise was intended to be binding, supported by the financial deposit and credible, clear terms, distinguishing it from mere marketing puff and equivalent to commitments upheld in Carlill. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶6
