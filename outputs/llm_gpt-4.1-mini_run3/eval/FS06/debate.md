# Debate transcript: Adeyemi v Harbourside Hotel Ltd

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] The hotel owed Ms Adeyemi a duty of care to take reasonable care for her belongings, as persons closely and directly affected by the hotel's acts are 'neighbours' under Lord Atkin's neighbour principle, establishing a duty despite the absence of a contractual relationship, per Donoghue v Stevenson [J01:p3]. — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A1.2** [I2] The exclusion notice on the bedroom door was not incorporated into the contract because the contract was made at reception before the notice was seen, following Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd where a similar contractual timing issue precluded incorporation of such terms [J42:p2]. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.3** [I3] Leaving the master key unattended breached the hotel's standard of care as it failed to exercise reasonable care in securing guest property, analogous to negligence by omission as per Olley, where the hotel was liable for negligence regarding keys [J42:p1]. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A1.4** [I4] The hotel cannot rely on the exclusion notice to exclude liability because the clause was not part of the contract and was not fairly brought to Ms Adeyemi's attention, consistent with Olley, holding such exclusion notices ineffective if introduced post-contract [J42:p2]. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.5** [I5] Ms Adeyemi had no actual or constructive notice of the exclusion clause as it was not communicated before or at the time of contract formation at reception, aligning with Parker v South Eastern Railway and Olley on the necessity of reasonable notice to be effective [J41:p2][J42:p2]. — *Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416, ¶2; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The hotel owed a duty of care to Ms Adeyemi to take reasonable care for her belongings since hotel guests are foreseeably affected by the hotel's acts and omissions; this aligns with the neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson establishing such a duty to avoid foreseeable harm to those closely affected by the defendant's conduct. — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C1.2** [I2] *(replies to A1.2)* The exclusion notice on the bedroom door was not incorporated into the contract since the contract was formed at reception before the guest saw the notice, paralleling Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd where similar timing precluded incorporation of such exclusion clauses. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C1.3** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* Leaving the master key unattended amounted to breach of the hotel's standard of care by neglecting reasonable security measures, consistent with Olley where negligence with room keys led to liability for loss of guest property. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C1.4** [I4] *(replies to A1.4)* The hotel cannot rely on the exclusion notice to exclude liability because it was not part of the contract and was not fairly brought to Ms Adeyemi's attention before contract formation, as held in Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd, rendering the exclusion ineffective. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C1.5** [I5] *(replies to A1.5)* Ms Adeyemi had no actual or constructive notice of the exclusion clause before or at the time of contracting; consistent with Parker v South Eastern Railway Co where incorporating unsigned terms depends on reasonable efforts to bring them to the party's attention, which did not occur here. — *Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416, ¶2; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* The hotel owed Ms Adeyemi a duty of care to take reasonable care for her belongings as the hotel's acts and omissions could foreseeably cause injury to guests, who are 'neighbors' under Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson, establishing such a duty even absent a contract, applying here where the hotel had control over guests' security and a foreseeable risk of property loss existed as Ms Adeyemi was a guest (F1, F5; J01:p3). — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A2.2** [I2] *(replies to C1.2)* The exclusion notice on the bedroom door was not incorporated into the contract as the contract was concluded at the reception desk prior to Ms Adeyemi seeing the notice in her room, like in Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd where similar timing prevented incorporation of exclusion clauses appearing after contract formation (F1, F2, F3; J42:p2). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.3** [I3] *(replies to C1.3)* The hotel breached its standard of care by leaving the master key unattended, a negligent omission analogous to Olley where the hotel was liable for negligence involving room keys, showing failure to take reasonable security precautions for guest property (F5; J42:p1). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶5
- **A2.4** [I4] *(replies to C1.4)* Reliance on the exclusion notice is legally ineffective as it was not part of the contract and was not fairly brought to Ms Adeyemi's attention before contracting; this follows Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd where similar exclusions were held ineffective for being post-contractual and unnoticed (F2, F3; J42:p2). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.5** [I5] *(replies to C1.5)* Ms Adeyemi had no actual or constructive notice of the exclusion terms to bind her contractually, matching Parker v South Eastern Railway which demands reasonable steps be taken to bring the terms to the party's attention before or at contract formation, which did not occur here (F2, F3; J41:p2, J42:p2). — *Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416, ¶2; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* The hotel owed no duty of care to Ms Adeyemi regarding the security of her belongings because the general neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson applies primarily to manufacturers and personal injury, not to hotel security, which may lack the necessary proximity and foreseeability in this context (J01:p3). — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3
- **C2.2** [I2] *(replies to A2.2)* The exclusion notice on the bedroom door was not incorporated into the contract because, as in Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd, the contract was concluded at reception before Ms Adeyemi saw the notice in her room, preventing incorporation of the exclusion clause (F1, F2, F3; J42:p2). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2
- **C2.3** [I3] *(replies to A2.3)* Leaving the master key unattended is not necessarily a breach of the hotel's standard of care if reasonable steps were taken generally; Olley shows negligence but is distinguishable by its particular facts and does not impose absolute liability for key handling in every case (F5; J42:p1). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶1
- **C2.4** [I4] *(replies to A2.4)* The hotel cannot rely on the exclusion notice to exclude liability because it was not part of the contract and was not fairly brought to Ms Adeyemi's attention before contract formation, rendering such exclusion ineffective as held in Olley (F2, F3; J42:p2). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2
- **C2.5** [I5] *(replies to A2.5)* Ms Adeyemi had no actual or constructive notice of the exclusion terms to bind her contractually because reasonable notice must be given before or at contract formation, which did not occur here as the terms were only visible after contracting, consistent with Parker v South Eastern Railway Co (F2, F3; J41:p2). — *Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416, ¶2
