# Debate transcript: Okafor v St Aldhelm's Hospital NHS Trust

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] The Trust owed Mr Okafor a duty of care to inform him of the 2% risk and alternative treatments because a doctor must take reasonable care to ensure a patient is aware of any material risks and reasonable alternatives, especially where the patient has a particular concern, here fine finger control critical to his career, which the doctor knew or should have known. This follows from the principle in Montgomery that the doctor's duty to disclose risks is patient-centred and requires disclosure of risks a reasonable patient or this particular patient would attach significance to (J06:p2; J06:p3; F3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.2** [I2] The Trust breached the standard of care by failing to warn Mr Okafor of the material 2% risk and alternative treatments. Montgomery abandons the Bolam test for risk disclosure, requiring doctors to inform patients of material risks as judged by a reasonable patient or individual patient considerations. The Trust's reliance on a body of medical opinion that did not routinely mention this risk is insufficient, as the Supreme Court rejected deferential medical opinion on non-disclosure of risks in Montgomery (J06:p2; J06:p3; F2). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2
- **A1.3** [I3] Mr Okafor did not give informed consent because he was not warned of a material risk or realistic alternative treatments which would have affected his decision. Chester confirms that non-disclosure of even a small but material risk vitiates consent, especially where the patient asserts they would have delayed or sought alternatives; this is factually mirrored by Okafor's evidence (F6; J07:p1; J06:p2). — Fact sheet ¶6; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2
- **A1.4** [I4] The failure to warn caused Mr Okafor's loss because, as in Chester, even if the injury would have occurred on a later date, the loss resulted from the lack of information denying the patient's autonomy to make an informed choice about timing and alternative treatment. Okafor's evidence that he would have delayed surgery and sought other options satisfies this causation principle (F5; F6; J07:p2; J07:p3). — Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶3

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The Trust did not owe a duty of care in respect of disclosure of the 2% risk and alternative treatments because a novel duty must be imposed incrementally and analogously with established duties, requiring foreseeability, proximity, and fairness; here, the duty to warn of such small risks is not clearly established as a legal obligation (J02:p3). — *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶3
- **C1.2** [I2] *(replies to A1.2)* The standard of care was not breached as the Trust acted in accordance with a responsible body of medical opinion in 2024 that did not routinely disclose this small 2% risk; under Bolam, this medical practice is not negligent even if others might disclose it (J04:p2; F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C1.3** [I2] *(replies to A1.2)* Additionally, the low probability of a 2% risk permits a reasonable practitioner to omit such a disclosure, mirroring how courts weigh small risks against the practicability of precautions (J12:p3). — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3
- **C1.4** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* Mr Okafor's consent was informed in line with established medical standards; Chester is distinguishable as it involved a clear causal link and a demonstrated patient reluctance to undergo surgery at that time, whereas here the risk was low and his career implications were not met with a definite refusal (J07:p2; F6). — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C1.5** [I4] *(replies to A1.4)* The causal link between the failure to warn and Mr Okafor's loss is too tenuous; as held in Bolitho, causation requires the hypothetical conduct to be non-negligent and likely to prevent the harm, which is not established here since the injury risk was inherent in the surgery (J05:p3; F6). — *Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority* [1998] AC 232, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* The Trust owed a duty of care to Mr Okafor because the doctor must ensure the patient is aware of material risks and reasonable alternatives, especially since Mr Okafor made clear his career depended on fine finger control, a fact known to the doctor, making the risk material to him specifically, per Montgomery (J06:p2; J06:p3; F3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.2** [I2] *(replies to C1.2)* The Trust breached the standard of care as Montgomery overruled Bolam for risk disclosure, requiring disclosure of material risks judged by a reasonable patient or by particular patient concerns, and the practice of not disclosing a 2% risk falls short, especially given the significance to Mr Okafor (J06:p2; J06:p3; F2; F7). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **A2.3** [I2] *(replies to C1.3)* Contrary to relying on low risk to omit warning, the 2% risk was material to Mr Okafor’s position due to his career reliance, and the protective standard adopted by the court considers patient vulnerability, making omission unreasonable here and distinguishable from low general risks like in Bolton (J06:p3; J13:p3; F3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; *Paris v Stepney Borough Council* [1951] AC 367, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.4** [I3] *(replies to C1.4)* Mr Okafor did not give informed consent as he was unaware of material risks and alternatives, and Chester confirms that a patient’s right to autonomy includes being warned of even low risks if they would affect consent timing, which Mr Okafor’s evidence supports (F6; J07:p1; J06:p2). — Fact sheet ¶6; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2
- **A2.5** [I4] *(replies to C1.5)* The failure to warn caused the loss because Chester established causation where the risk of injury was within the scope of the warned risk and the patient was deprived of the choice to delay surgery or seek alternatives, which Mr Okafor would have done (F5; F6; J07:p2; J07:p3). — Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶3

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* The Trust did not owe a duty of care to warn Mr Okafor of the 2% risk and alternative treatments under established legal principles, as imposing such a duty in this novel context requires incremental development with foreseeability, proximity and fairness, which here are not satisfied since the risk is very small and the duty to warn of all possible alternatives is not clearly established in law, per Caparo. — *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶3
- **C2.2** [I2] *(replies to A2.2)* The standard of care was not breached because it was common medical practice in 2024, supported by a responsible body of medical opinion, not to routinely disclose the 2% risk, and thus in accordance with Bolam test the Trust acted without negligence despite alternative medical opinion. — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C2.3** [I2] *(replies to A2.3)* In addition, the low probability (2%) constitutes a very small risk which a reasonable person may disregard, weighing the likelihood and practicability of precautions, and this justifies omission of the warning under the standard of care analysis in Bolton v Stone. — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2
- **C2.4** [I3] *(replies to A2.4)* Mr Okafor gave informed consent as under Chester the duty to warn applies where refusal or delay to consent is established, which here is speculative since Mr Okafor admits he might have had the surgery eventually; thus Chester is distinguishable as it required a clear causal consent refusal and a direct link that is lacking here. — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C2.5** [I4] *(replies to A2.5)* The failure to warn did not cause the loss since Bolitho requires the hypothetical alternative conduct to be non-negligent and likely to avoid harm, which is unproven here because the risk was intrinsic to the surgery and the missed warning would not have changed the medical risk outcome. — *Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority* [1998] AC 232, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
