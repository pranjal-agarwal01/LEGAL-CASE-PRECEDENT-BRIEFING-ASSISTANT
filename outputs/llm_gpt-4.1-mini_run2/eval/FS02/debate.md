# Debate transcript: Okafor v St Aldhelm's Hospital NHS Trust

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] St Aldhelm's Hospital owed Mr Okafor a duty of care to inform him of material risks, as established by Montgomery, which requires doctors to ensure patients are aware of risks a reasonable person in their position would find significant, or that the doctor knows are significant to that particular patient, here Mr Okafor who explicitly stated his career depended on fine finger control (F3,J06:p2,p3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.2** [I2] The Trust breached the standard of care by failing to disclose the roughly 2% risk of nerve root damage and the alternative of further physiotherapy and steroid injections. Montgomery departs from Bolam in risk disclosure, imposing an objective duty to inform of material risks and alternatives, which includes Mr Okafor's specific situation (F2,F3,J06:p1,p2). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶1; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.3** [I3] The adequacy of information provided to Mr Okafor is assessed under informed consent as per Montgomery, which rejects the Bolam test for disclosure of risks and requires respect for patient autonomy, here disregarded by failing to disclose the material risk and alternative treatments (J06:p2,p3,F2,F3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.4** [I4] The failure to warn causally contributed to Mr Okafor's loss of fine motor control and professional damage, as the risk that materialised was precisely that which the doctor had a duty to warn about under Chester, where causation was satisfied despite a likelihood of the same operation later; Mr Okafor stated he would have delayed surgery and sought alternative treatments (F5,F6,J07:p1,p2). — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The Trust did not owe a duty to disclose the 2% risk because a responsible body of spinal surgeons in 2024 did not routinely mention this low risk, aligning with the Bolam standard rejected by Montgomery only for disclosure, not for duty existence; thus, the Trust's practice was not unreasonable in context (J04:p2, J06:p3, F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C1.2** [I2] *(replies to A1.2)* The Trust's conduct conforms to a responsible medical practice where many surgeons did not disclose such a 2% risk or alternatives routinely, which under Bolam remains authoritative for standard of care in treatment and disclosure not mandated by Montgomery; hence, no breach arose (J04:p2, F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C1.3** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* Montgomery recognizes a narrow therapeutic exception protecting doctors from disclosure when disclosure would be seriously detrimental to the patient’s health or decision-making; the Trust might contend that nondisclosure fell within this exception given the small but realistic risk and standard practice, thus the adequacy is not established merely by patient's desire for full disclosure (J06:p3, J04:p2, F7). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C1.4** [I4] *(replies to A1.4)* Causation fails because Mr Okafor would have eventually undergone the surgery, as he admits (F6), and the injury was due to the inherent surgical risk, not negligent treatment; Chester modifies but-for only narrowly to protect autonomy where timing is crucial, which is not applicable here since the injury and timing would not differ materially (J07:p2, J07:p1, F6). — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* The Trust owed Mr Okafor a duty to inform him of material risks, as Montgomery establishes that doctors must ensure patients know risks a reasonable person in the patient's position would find significant, or that the doctor knows are significant to that patient. Here Mr Okafor explicitly told Mr Lyle his career depended on fine finger control, making the 2% risk material to him (F3, J06:p2, J06:p3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.2** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* The opponent's reliance on Bolam for existence of duty is misconceived; Montgomery rejects Bolam only for risk disclosure standard but assumes the duty to inform exists where there is a proximate relationship, as here. Hence the Trust's failure to warn breaches its duty (J06:p3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶7
- **A2.3** [I2] *(replies to C1.2)* The Trust breached the standard of care by failing to disclose the material 2% risk and alternative of further physiotherapy, as Montgomery departs from Bolam and requires informing patients of material risks and reasonable alternatives, especially when the patient qualifies their concerns, here Mr Okafor as to fine finger control (F2, F3, J06:p1, J06:p2). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶1; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.4** [I2] *(replies to C1.2)* The opponent's reliance on a responsible body practice not routinely disclosing the 2% risk is contradicted by Montgomery's rejection of Bolam for risk disclosure, making responsible practice irrelevant if it ignores material risks to this patient (J06:p2, J06:p3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶7
- **A2.5** [I3] *(replies to C1.3)* Montgomery governs adequacy of information under informed consent, rejecting Bolam's deference to medical opinion in risk disclosure. The Trust’s failure to inform violated the claimant’s right to autonomy and informed choice, disregarding his specific expressed concerns (J06:p2, J06:p3, F2, F3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.6** [I3] *(replies to C1.3)* The therapeutic exception invoked by the Trust is narrow and cannot exclude disclosure when the patient’s autonomous choice is impaired; here Mr Okafor’s career dependency on fine motor control triggers disclosure—no evidence the risk disclosure would harm him seriously (J06:p3, F3, F7). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶7
- **A2.7** [I4] *(replies to C1.4)* Causation is satisfied under Chester because the injury was from the risk the Trust had a duty to warn about. Mr Okafor stated he would have deferred surgery and sought other treatment, so the timing mattered and the Trust’s breach caused loss of opportunity to avoid or delay the damage (F5, F6, J07:p1, J07:p2). — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6
- **A2.8** [I4] *(replies to C1.4)* The opponent misapplies Chester’s narrow modification of but-for causation; it applies when risk timing affects autonomy and decision-making, as here where the Claimant would have postponed surgery, the injury timing and consequent professional loss are causally linked to failure to warn (J07:p1, J07:p2, F6, F5). — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6; Fact sheet ¶5

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* The duty to disclose a risk does not arise merely from the patient's vulnerability or career reliance but is conditioned by the professional practice accepted by a responsible body of medical opinion. Here, the Trust's practice conforms to that responsible body, so no duty to warn of the 2% risk was breached; Montgomery rejects Bolam only for the standard of disclosure, not for the establishment of duty itself (J04:p2, J06:p3, F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C2.2** [I2] *(replies to A2.3)* The Trust did not breach the standard of care because, as per Bolam, the disclosure of such a small risk and alternative treatments is not mandated if a responsible body of spinal surgeons do not routinely disclose them; Montgomery's departure from Bolam applies only to risk disclosure standard, not to override an accepted responsible practice, especially given the 2% risk is low and commonly undisclosed by peers (J04:p2, F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C2.3** [I3] *(replies to A2.5)* The adequacy of information disclosure is subject to the narrow therapeutic exception under Montgomery, which permits withholding information where disclosure would likely cause serious harm to the patient's health. Given no evidence shows disclosure here would harm Mr Okafor's health and the Trust followed standard practice, the exception applies and the information provision was adequate (J06:p3, J04:p2, F7). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C2.4** [I4] *(replies to A2.7)* On causation, Chester's modification of but-for causation is narrowly confined to cases where timing of injury affects the autonomy interest. Since Mr Okafor admits he would have ultimately had the surgery and the injury risk remained unchanged, the failure to warn did not cause his loss—his injury was an inherent surgical risk, not due to the Trust's omission (J07:p2, J07:p1, F6). — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶6
