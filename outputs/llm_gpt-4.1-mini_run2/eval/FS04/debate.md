# Debate transcript: Rowe v Skyline Terraces Ltd

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] The contract between Mr Rowe and Skyline Terraces Ltd is frustrated because the foundation of the contract was the fireworks display, which was cancelled. The parties' common purpose was for Mr Rowe and his guests to view the fireworks from the terrace, as shown by Skyline's advertising and their emails promising 'front-row seats' for the display (F2). This aligns with Krell v Henry where the cancellation of an event that formed the foundation of the contract frustrated the contract, releasing the party from further obligations (J37:p2, J37:p3). — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶2; *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The contract was not frustrated as the cancellation of the fireworks did not make performance impossible, but only less attractive. The terrace, bar, and staff remained available for use, so the contract's fundamental purpose—exclusive use of the terrace with bar service—could still be fulfilled (F3, F5). This aligns with Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton where the contract was not frustrated even after cancellation of part of the event since the chartered boat could still be used (J38:p2). — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C1.2** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The cancellation of the fireworks affected only Mr Rowe's personal commercial purpose and did not destroy a shared common foundation of the contract. In Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton, it was held the frustration doctrine does not apply where the event defeats only one party's purpose or merely reduces profitability (J38:p3, F6). — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C1.3** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The contract's written terms described only exclusive use of the terrace from 6pm to midnight with bar service, without mention of the fireworks, indicating the fireworks were not part of the contractual basis (F3). This distinguishes Krell v Henry where the event was the foundation of the contract despite absence from the writing because both parties recognised it as such (J37:p1). — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C1.4** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* Frustration requires that contractual obligations become radically different from those undertaken without default by either party. Here, only inconvenience or loss of attraction occurred, not impossibility or fundamental change, mirroring the ruling in Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham where increased difficulty or cost did not amount to frustration (J39:p2, J39:p3, F6). — *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶2; *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.3)* The foundation of the contract was the fireworks display, as both parties knew viewing the fireworks was the basis of the agreement (F2). This mirrors Krell v Henry where the contract was frustrated because the event was central to the contract, and its cancellation released the party from payment obligations despite performance being physically possible (J37:p1, J37:p2, J37:p3). — Fact sheet ¶2; *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶1; *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶2; *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶3
- **A2.2** [I1] *(replies to C1.2)* The fireworks cancellation destroyed the common foundation of the contract, not merely Mr Rowe's individual purpose, contrary to Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton where the event was only the hirer's own purpose (J38:p2, J38:p3; F2). Here, Skyline's advertising and promises show a shared basis, unlike in Herne Bay (F2). — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶2; *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2
- **A2.3** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* While the terrace and bar remained available, the contract was for the terrace as a vantage point to view the fireworks (F2), and their cancellation so fundamentally changed the contractual purpose as to frustrate the contract, similar to the destruction of the music hall in Taylor v Caldwell, discharging the contract when the thing contracted for ceased to exist (J36:p2, J36:p3; F4). — Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶2; *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶3
- **A2.4** [I1] *(replies to C1.4)* Frustration requires a radical difference in obligation, not just increased burden or inconvenience. The cancellation here made the contractual basis impossible, not merely less attractive, contrasting with Davis Contractors where delay and cost increase did not frustrate (J39:p2, J39:p3; F4, F6). — *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶2; *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.3)* The contract was not frustrated because the terrace and related services remained available and usable, meaning the contract's core obligation—providing exclusive use of the terrace with bar service—was still performable, aligning with Herne Bay where frustration was denied as the chartered steamboat's use remained possible despite cancellation of the naval review (J38:p2; F3, F5). — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- **C2.2** [I1] *(replies to A2.2)* Cancellation of the fireworks solely affected Mr Rowe's commercial purpose and did not destroy the contractual foundation shared by both parties, which was the terrace hire itself, not the fireworks display; this is supported by Herne Bay where frustration was rejected as the frustrating event defeated only the hirer's purpose (J38:p3; F6). — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C2.3** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* The hire agreement's written terms, which only mention exclusive terrace use and bar service, exclude the fireworks from the contractual basis, distinguishing Krell where both parties expressly recognised the cancelled event as central; thus, no common foundation for frustration exists here (J37:p1; F3). — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C2.4** [I1] *(replies to A2.4)* Frustration requires the contractual obligations to become radically different, not merely less attractive or profitable. Here, only inconvenience or loss of enjoyment occurred; this parallels Davis Contractors where delays and extra costs did not frustrate despite increased burdens (J39:p2, J39:p3; F6). — *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶2; *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
