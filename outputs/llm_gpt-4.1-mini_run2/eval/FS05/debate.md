# Debate transcript: Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd v SwiftHaul Couriers Ltd

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] SwiftHaul owed a duty of care to Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd because the bakery was directly affected by SwiftHaul's act of delivering the parcel late, fulfilling Lord Atkin's neighbour principle that one must avoid acts foreseeably injurious to those closely affected (J01:p3; F2, F4). The delivery service entails a proximate relationship and foreseeability of harm through delay, establishing a duty under established negligence principles (J19:p2). — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3; *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4
- **A1.2** [I2] SwiftHaul breached its standard of care by sending the parcel to the wrong depot, as an objectively reasonable courier would not commit such a fundamental error in guaranteed next-day delivery (J11:p3; F4). This lapse caused an 11-day delay and consequent losses, illustrating failure to meet the care expected from a competent carrier (J11:p2; F4). — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶3; *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4
- **A1.3** [I3] The exclusion clause cannot bar recovery because Kaur Artisan Bakery's manager ticked the terms without knowledge of the critical nature of the parcel or the special losses, so the clause should be construed narrowly against SwiftHaul. According to principles in Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd, onerous clauses excluding loss of profits must be clearly brought to the claimant's attention before the contract is made, which is not evidenced here (J42:p2; F3). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.4** [I4] Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd can recover the exceptional loss from the lost wedding contract despite SwiftHaul's lack of knowledge because such loss arises naturally from the breach, akin to the recoverable losses in Victoria Laundry where special contracts known to the defendant were considered (J35:p2). Here, while SwiftHaul had no actual knowledge, the bakery's failure to notify does not preclude the claim for foreseeable losses arising from the breach (F5, F6). — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* No duty of care arises beyond contractual obligations here, as SwiftHaul had no knowledge of any special circumstances making the loss foreseeable beyond the ordinary delivery. Caparo establishes that duty of care requires sufficient proximity and reasonable foreseeability of harm specific to the claimant, which is lacking as the bakery did not inform SwiftHaul that delay would cause serious loss (J02:p2; F2, F6). — *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C1.2** [I2] *(replies to A1.2)* SwiftHaul did not breach its standard of care by the mere occurrence of delay. The fact of error does not establish breach if the reasonable precautions and standard practices of the courier were followed, which is absent from the facts. No evidence shows that sending to the wrong depot was careless as opposed to an isolated mistake within normal business risk (J12:p3; F4). — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4
- **C1.3** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* The exclusion clause excluding liability for loss of profits and business is valid and binding as the bakery's manager objectively accepted these terms by ticking the box. L'Estrange confirms that signing (or equivalent acceptance) binds a party regardless of actual knowledge, absent fraud or misrepresentation, and the clause's clear wording covers these losses in this commercial contract (J40:p2; F3). — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C1.4** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* Olley is distinguishable since here the terms were incorporated proactively upon booking, unlike a notice discovered only after contract conclusion. Therefore, the exclusion clause's incorporation stands and bars the bakery's claim for lost profits and contracts (J40:p2; F3). — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C1.5** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* Photo Production establishes that a clearly worded exclusion clause will be enforced to allocate commercial risk between parties of equal bargaining power, supporting SwiftHaul's protection against extensive loss claims (J44:p2; F3). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C1.6** [I4] *(replies to A1.4)* The lost wedding contract is not recoverable as SwiftHaul was unaware of this special contract and therefore could not have contemplated the loss. Hadley v Baxendale confirms that only losses arising naturally or known to parties at contracting time are recoverable, and here the special circumstances were not communicated (J34:p2; F5, F6). — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* SwiftHaul owed a duty of care to the bakery as it was foreseeable that a delayed delivery could directly affect the bakery's operation, fulfilling Lord Atkin's neighbour principle that liability arises for foreseeable injury to those closely affected by one's acts, particularly in a direct contractual service relationship such as delivery (J01:p3; J19:p2; F2, F4). — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3; *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4
- **A2.2** [I2] *(replies to C1.2)* The standard of care was breached because sending the parcel to the wrong depot was a fundamental error inconsistent with the objective standard of a reasonably competent courier delivering guaranteed next-day services, as established in Nettleship v Weston (J11:p3; J11:p2; F4). — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶3; *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4
- **A2.3** [I3] *(replies to C1.3)* The exclusion clause should be construed narrowly against SwiftHaul, especially as the bakery was unaware of the consequences and the clause is onerous; excluding loss of profits and contracts must have been clearly brought to the bakery's attention before contract formation, per Olley, and this is not evidenced by merely ticking a box online without explicit notice of such exclusion (J42:p2; F3). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.4** [I4] *(replies to C1.6)* The lost wedding contract loss is recoverable given it resulted from breach naturally and foreseeably as with Victoria Laundry, since SwiftHaul's lack of knowledge of the special contract does not bar recovery of losses the bakery reasonably suffered from delayed delivery (J35:p2; F5, F6). — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* No duty of care arises outside the contract here, as SwiftHaul had no knowledge of the special consequences of delay beyond normal delivery risk, so the proximity required under Caparo is absent (J02:p2; F2, F6). — *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C2.2** [I2] *(replies to A2.2)* Sending the parcel to the wrong depot may be a mistake, but not necessarily a breach of the standard of care; Bolton v Stone shows that small probability or isolated risk without further evidence of negligence does not establish breach (J12:p3; F4). — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4
- **C2.3** [I3] *(replies to A2.3)* The bakery is bound by the exclusion clause as they accepted it by ticking the box online; L'Estrange confirms a party is bound by signed terms regardless of knowledge, which here includes loss of profits and contracts due to clear wording in a commercial contract (J40:p2; F3). — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C2.4** [I3] *(replies to A2.3)* Olley is distinguishable because the exclusion clause was incorporated at the time of booking, not by a notice after contract formation; here the bakery had constructive knowledge of the clause through online acceptance unlike in Olley (F3). — Fact sheet ¶3
- **C2.5** [I3] *(replies to A2.3)* Photo Production confirms that in commercial contracts between parties of equal bargaining power, clear exclusion clauses allocating risk will be upheld even if they cover fundamental breaches (J44:p2; F3). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **C2.6** [I4] *(replies to A2.4)* The lost wedding contract is not recoverable as SwiftHaul was unaware of this special contract and could not have contemplated this loss at contracting; Hadley v Baxendale holds that losses which arise from unknown special circumstances are not recoverable (J34:p2; F5, F6). — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6
