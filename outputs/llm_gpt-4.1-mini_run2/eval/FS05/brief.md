# Brief: Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd v SwiftHaul Couriers Ltd

**Case** FS05 · **Acting for** Claimant · **Prepared** 15 November 2026 · **Mode** llm:foundry

> Academic demonstration only. This is not legal advice. It was produced by a multi-agent prototype from a synthetic fact sheet and a small teaching corpus of paraphrased judgment summaries; verify any authority against the official law report.

## Summary

- Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd contracted with SwiftHaul for guaranteed next-day delivery of a critical electronic part for their industrial oven, crucial to their business operations (F2, F4). SwiftHaul negligently sent the parcel to the wrong depot, causing an eleven-day delay and significant loss of profits, including a special contract the bakery lost (F5). While SwiftHaul relies on an exclusion clause for loss of profit or business, the bakery argues it was unaware of the clause's significance and that such exclusions must be clearly communicated (F3, J42:p2). The bakery also contends that the exceptional lost contract should be recoverable despite SwiftHaul's lack of knowledge, as it naturally arises from the breach. — Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶3; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2

## Issues

1. **I1.** Did SwiftHaul owe a duty of care to Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd in performing the delivery contract?
2. **I2.** Was SwiftHaul's standard of care in delivering the parcel breached by sending it to the wrong depot causing delay?
3. **I3.** Are Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd's lost profits and lost contract recoverable given the exclusion clause in SwiftHaul's terms?
4. **I4.** Could Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd recover loss for the exceptional wedding contract despite SwiftHaul's lack of knowledge about it?

## Key facts

- The bakery's industrial oven failed on 3 September 2025 and needed the controller board repaired at the manufacturer's workshop in Birmingham. — Fact sheet ¶1
- On 4 September 2025, the bakery booked SwiftHaul's guaranteed next-day delivery service describing the parcel as 'electronic component' without informing about its inability to trade without it. — Fact sheet ¶2
- The bakery accepted SwiftHaul's terms and conditions including clause 9 which excludes liability for any loss of profit, business, or contracts arising from delay. — Fact sheet ¶3
- SwiftHaul mistakenly sent the parcel to the wrong depot and delivered it eleven days late on 15 September 2025. — Fact sheet ¶4
- The bakery was unable to bake for twelve days, losing about £18,000 in ordinary trade profits and a £40,000 contract for a celebrity wedding, which SwiftHaul did not know about. — Fact sheet ¶5
- SwiftHaul argues it could not have contemplated the bakery's inability to trade or the exceptional wedding contract, and relies on clause 9 to exclude liability for these losses. — Fact sheet ¶6
- The bakery claims damages for breach of contract covering its lost profits and lost wedding contract due to the late delivery. — Fact sheet ¶7

## I1. Did SwiftHaul owe a duty of care to Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd in performing the delivery contract?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 | A duty of care arises to avoid acts foreseeably injurious to closely affected persons, established by Lord Atkin's neighbour principle. | *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3 |
| *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4 | Existing negligence principles apply where positive acts create foreseeable risk of injury; here a duty arises in delivery causing delay. | *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605 | Duty of care requires proximity and reasonable foreseeability of harm specific to claimant; lacking where defendant unaware of special circumstances. | *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶2 |

**Argument for the client**

- SwiftHaul owed a duty of care as delay foreseeably affected the bakery, fulfilling the neighbour principle, with a proximate delivery service relationship (J01:p3; J19:p2; F2, F4). — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3; *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4

**Argument for the other side**

- SwiftHaul owed no duty of care beyond the contract because it lacked knowledge of any special circumstances making loss foreseeable; proximity and foreseeability specific to the bakery are absent (J02:p2; F2, F6). — *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6

**Assessment**

- The duty of care likely exists in contract delivery given direct foreseeability of harm from delayed delivery and close relationship, aligning with established negligence principles (J01,p3; J19,p2). While Caparo requires proximity, the bakery's reliance on guaranteed next-day service and the direct effect of delay likely satisfies this. — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3; *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2; *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶6

## I2. Was SwiftHaul's standard of care in delivering the parcel breached by sending it to the wrong depot causing delay?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691 | The standard of care is objective; an actor is held to the reasonable competent standard regardless of personal limitations. | *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶3 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850 | Breach requires weighing of likelihood and practicability; a small probability or isolated risk usually does not constitute breach. | *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- Sending the parcel to the wrong depot was a fundamental error inconsistent with the objective standard of a reasonably competent courier in guaranteed next-day delivery (J11:p3; J11:p2; F4). — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶3; *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4

**Argument for the other side**

- The isolated delivery error may not breach the standard of care if reasonable precautions and standard business practices were followed; small risk without evidence of negligence suffices to deny breach (J12:p3; F4). — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4

**Assessment**

- SwiftHaul likely breached the standard of care by mis-routing a guaranteed next-day delivery, given the objective competent courier standard (J11:p3). The opposing argument depends on absence of proof of negligence, but a fundamental error causing 11-day delay undermines such defense (J12:p3). — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶3; *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4

## I3. Are Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd's lost profits and lost contract recoverable given the exclusion clause in SwiftHaul's terms?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532 | Exclusion clauses must be brought to attention before contract formation; onerous clauses excluding loss must be clearly communicated to be effective. | *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394 | Signing or equivalent acceptance binds a party to exclusion clauses regardless of knowledge, absent fraud or misrepresentation. | *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2 |
| *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827 | Clear, unambiguous exclusion clauses are upheld in commercial contracts to allocate risks, even for fundamental breaches. | *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶2 |

**Argument for the client**

- The exclusion clause should be construed narrowly against SwiftHaul as an onerous clause excluding loss of profits and contracts must be clearly brought to the bakery's attention before contract formation, which is not demonstrated by mere online ticking (J42:p2; F3). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

**Argument for the other side**

- The bakery accepted the exclusion clause by ticking the box, binding them regardless of knowledge per L'Estrange, and the clause's clear wording excludes lost profits and contracts (J40:p2; F3). — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- Photo Production confirms that in commercial contracts between parties with equal bargaining power, clear and properly incorporated exclusion clauses are enforced even for fundamental breaches (J44:p2; F3). — *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

**Assessment**

- The bakery's acceptance by ticking the box supports incorporation of the exclusion clause under L'Estrange and Photo Production, favoring enforcement (J40:p2; J44:p2). However, the argument that such onerous clauses require explicit prior notice may favor narrow construction against SwiftHaul (J42:p2). The balance leans to enforcement given commercial context and clearness of clause (F3). — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

## I4. Could Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd recover loss for the exceptional wedding contract despite SwiftHaul's lack of knowledge about it?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528 | Losses reasonably foreseeable from breach, judged by knowledge at contracting, are recoverable; exceptional known contracts' losses are compensable. | *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341 | Losses from special circumstances unknown to defendant are not recoverable as not reasonably foreseeable. | *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2 |

**Argument for the client**

- The lost wedding contract loss is recoverable as it naturally arises from the breach and relates to loss reasonably foreseeable by analogy with Victoria Laundry, even if SwiftHaul lacked actual knowledge (J35:p2; F5, F6). — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6

**Argument for the other side**

- The exceptional lost contract is not recoverable since SwiftHaul was unaware and could not have contemplated it; Hadley v Baxendale requires special losses to be communicated to recover (J34:p2; F5, F6). — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6

**Assessment**

- Hadley v Baxendale strongly supports barring recovery of losses from unknown special circumstances like the wedding contract (J34:p2). Victoria Laundry allows recovery of ordinary lost profits and known special contracts, which does not apply here due to SwiftHaul's ignorance. The opposing view aligns with established remoteness principles and likely prevails. — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6

## Conclusion

- SwiftHaul owed a duty of care to Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd due to the direct and foreseeable risk from delayed delivery in a contractual carrier relationship (J01:p3; J19:p2). — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3; *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2
- SwiftHaul breached the standard of care by misrouting the parcel, resulting in an 11-day delay and significant losses (J11:p3; F4). — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4
- However, the exclusion clause in SwiftHaul's terms likely bars recovery of lost profits and contracts, as the bakery accepted these terms online and the clause is clear and enforceable in commercial contexts (J40:p2; J44:p2; F3). — *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- Losses from the exceptional wedding contract are probably unrecoverable as SwiftHaul lacked knowledge of the special circumstances, consistent with Hadley v Baxendale (J34:p2; F5, F6). — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶6
