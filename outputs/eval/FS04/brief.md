## Issues

1. **I1.** Was the contract frustrated by a supervening event that destroyed its common foundation?

## Key facts

- On 10 May 2025 Mr Rowe agreed to hire Skyline's rooftop terrace for the evening of 5 July 2025 for £12,000 and paid a £4,000 deposit. — Fact sheet ¶1
- Skyline's online listing advertised the terrace as "the best view in London of the Thames Victory Fireworks", a display planned to celebrate the city hosting a sporting final. Mr Rowe's emails to Skyline said his guests were coming "to watch the fireworks", and Skyline's reply promised "front-row seats for the display". — Fact sheet ¶2
- The written hire agreement described only "exclusive use of the terrace, 6pm to midnight, with bar service" and did not mention the fireworks. — Fact sheet ¶3
- On 28 June 2025 the council cancelled the fireworks display after a safety review. — Fact sheet ¶4
- The terrace, bar and staff remained available on 5 July 2025. On 29 June 2025 Mr Rowe cancelled his event and asked for his deposit back. — Fact sheet ¶5
- Skyline refuses to return the deposit and claims the £8,000 balance. It says the terrace could still be used, that the fireworks were only Mr Rowe's own commercial purpose, and that the event simply became less attractive. — Fact sheet ¶6

## I1. Was the contract frustrated by a supervening event that destroyed its common foundation?

**Argument for the client**

- Krell v Henry [1903] 2 KB 740 supports Thomas Rowe on this issue: A contract may be frustrated not only by physical impossibility but where a supervening event destroys a state of affairs which both parties regarded as the basis of the contract, even if literal performance remains possible. That reasoning applies here: On 10 May 2025 Mr Rowe agreed to hire Skyline's rooftop terrace for the evening of 5 July 2025 for £12,000 and paid a £4,000 deposit. — *Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1
- Taylor v Caldwell (1863) 3 B & S 826 supports Thomas Rowe on this issue: Where performance depends on the continued existence of a specific thing, the destruction of that thing without fault discharges the contract. That reasoning applies here: The terrace, bar and staff remained available on 5 July 2025. On 29 June 2025 Mr Rowe cancelled his event and asked for his deposit back. — *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council is distinguishable on its facts: Contractors agreed to build 78 houses for a fixed price within eight months. By contrast, in this case: The written hire agreement described only "exclusive use of the terrace, 6pm to midnight, with bar service" and did not mention the fireworks. — *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton is distinguishable on its facts: Hutton chartered a steamboat to take paying passengers to see the naval review at Spithead and to cruise round the fleet. By contrast, in this case: On 28 June 2025 the council cancelled the fireworks display after a safety review. — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶1; Fact sheet ¶4

**Argument for the other side**

- Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council [1956] AC 696 supports Skyline Terraces Ltd on this issue: Frustration occurs only where, without default of either party, a contractual obligation has become incapable of being performed because the circumstances would render it a thing radically different from that which was undertaken. That reasoning applies here: Skyline refuses to return the deposit and claims the £8,000 balance. It says the terrace could still be used, that the fireworks were only Mr Rowe's own commercial purpose, and that the event simply became less attractive. — *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton [1903] 2 KB 683 supports Skyline Terraces Ltd on this issue: Frustration does not apply where the event defeats only one party's purpose or makes the bargain less profitable; the risk of the venture falling short lies on the party who undertook it. That reasoning applies here: The terrace, bar and staff remained available on 5 July 2025. On 29 June 2025 Mr Rowe cancelled his event and asked for his deposit back. — *Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
