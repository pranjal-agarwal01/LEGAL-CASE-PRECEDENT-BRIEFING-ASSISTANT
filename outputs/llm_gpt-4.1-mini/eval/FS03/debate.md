# Debate transcript: Hughes v PeakFit Ltd

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 9

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] The advertisement by PeakFit constituted a legally binding offer because it was clear, definite and demonstrated an intention to be bound by placing £100,000 with Barclays to show seriousness, satisfying the criteria of a unilateral offer as in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. The performance of the conditions by Ms Hughes, logging all workouts and weighing in as required, amounted to acceptance and consideration without needing notification. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶1; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.2** [I2] PeakFit’s promise to pay £2,000 is enforceable and not mere marketing puff, as evidenced by the deposit with Barclays, paralleling Carlill's case where the deposit showed intention to create a legal relationship. Unlike domestic cases such as Balfour v Balfour, this is a commercial promotion with clear monetary stakes and a defined promise to pay upon performance of stipulated conditions. — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶1; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571, ¶2; *Merritt v Merritt* [1970] 1 WLR 1211, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.3** [I3] PeakFit's unilateral right to withdraw promotions not exercised before 15 April does not affect the contract's validity because the contract was concluded by performance upon Ms Hughes completing the conditions by that date, and terms that permit withdrawal must be part of the contract at formation. Analogous to Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd, such a term must be known and accepted at contract conclusion, which Ms Hughes was not effectively bound by until 15 April when performance was complete. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶2; *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5; Fact sheet ¶3

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* The PeakFit advertisement should be regarded as an invitation to treat, not a binding offer, as in Partridge v Crittenden and Fisher v Bell, where advertisements and displays were not offers due to the impracticality of contracting with unlimited respondents, akin to PeakFit's expectation that thousands would participate without intention to contract with all. This distinction applies as PeakFit did not intend to be bound to pay every participant, indicating the ad lacked the definiteness required for an offer.  — *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C1.2** [I2] *(replies to A1.2)* The promise to pay £2,000 is unenforceable as a binding contract because it amounts to mere marketing puff, lacking intention to create legal relations, akin to the domestic context presumption in Balfour v Balfour, especially given PeakFit's expressed view that the advertisement was promotional and not a contractual commitment, contrasting with the commercial seriousness in Carlill.  — *Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C1.3** [I3] *(replies to A1.3)* PeakFit's terms allowing withdrawal of promotions before 15 April effectively reserved the right to terminate any contractual offer before acceptance, and as Ms Hughes did not notify her participation, no contract was concluded before withdrawal could occur; thus, Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd does not validate contract formation despite unseen terms at contract conclusion.  — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶5

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* The advertisement by PeakFit constituted a a legally binding unilateral offer as in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, where the deposit of £100,000 with Barclays showed intention to be bound, and Ms Hughes accepted by performing the stipulated conditions without needing to notify acceptance, unlike in Partridge and Fisher where advertisements were mere invitations to treat due to lacking intention and definiteness (J23:p1; J23:p2; J23:p3; cf. J24:p2; J50:p3; F1; F3). — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶1; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶3; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.2** [I2] *(replies to C1.2)* PeakFit's promise is enforceable and not mere marketing puff, as the deposit with Barclays demonstrates commercial sincerity and intention to create legal relations, distinguishing it from Balfour v Balfour's domestic presumption; this commercial context aligns with Carlill's approach where the promise was definite and performance by Ms Hughes completed acceptance (J23:p2; J23:p1; J29:p2; F1; F3; F6). — *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶2; *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256, ¶1; *Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶6
- **A2.3** [I3] *(replies to C1.3)* The contract was concluded by Ms Hughes' performance on 15 April, thus the right to withdraw promotions before that date was ineffective as it was not exercised; consistent with Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd, terms allowing withdrawal must be known or incorporated before or at contract formation, which did not occur as Ms Hughes accepted by complete performance, not by notification (J42:p3; F3; F5). — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* The advertisement was an invitation to treat, not a binding offer, as in Partridge v Crittenden where the advertisement was held not an offer but an invitation to treat due to the impracticality of contracting with all respondents; similarly, Fisher v Bell established that goods displayed with a price are invitations to treat, reinforcing that PeakFit's ad cannot be an offer given the large number of expected participants and lack of intention to contract with each. — *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204, ¶2; *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶1; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C2.2** [I2] *(replies to A2.2)* The promise to pay £2,000 is unenforceable as it constitutes mere marketing puff without intention to create legal relations, paralleling the presumption in Balfour v Balfour that social or non-commercial promises lack such intention; here PeakFit clearly saw the advertisement as a promotion, not a legal commitment, distinguishing it from Carlill where genuine intention was shown. — *Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6; Fact sheet ¶1
- **C2.3** [I3] *(replies to A2.3)* PeakFit's term allowing withdrawal of promotions affects contract validity as such terms must be incorporated prior to or at contract formation; Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd holds that terms brought to notice only after contract formation are not incorporated, and since Ms Hughes accepted by performance without notification, the withdrawal term operated effectively to negate contract formation given PeakFit's non-acceptance stance. — *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
