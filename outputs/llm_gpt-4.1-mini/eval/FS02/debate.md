# Debate transcript: Okafor v St Aldhelm's Hospital NHS Trust

Mode: llm:foundry · LLM calls: 10

## Round 1: Argument Agent (client)

- **A1.1** [I1] St Aldhelm's Hospital NHS Trust owed Mr Okafor a duty of care to take reasonable care that he was aware of material risks and reasonable alternative treatments before consenting to surgery, as established in Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board where the Supreme Court held such duty in medical contexts to protect patient autonomy (J06:p2). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2
- **A1.2** [I1] The duty arises from the foreseeability that Mr Okafor would be directly affected by failure to inform him of such material risks, following Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson which underpins the modern negligence framework (J01:p3) and applies to doctors owing duty of care to patients. — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3
- **A1.3** [I2] Mr Lyle breached the standard of care by failing to warn Mr Okafor of the roughly 2% risk and reasonable alternatives, as Montgomery makes clear that doctors must disclose any material risks and alternatives relevant to the patient’s decision (J06:p2). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2
- **A1.4** [I3] The risk of nerve damage was a material risk requiring disclosure because a reasonable person in Mr Okafor's position, whose career depended on fine left-hand control, would attach significance to the risk, meeting the test laid down in Montgomery (J06:p3; F3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A1.5** [I4] The failure to warn caused Mr Okafor's loss notwithstanding he might eventually have had surgery, as Chester v Afshar held that injury from a risk a doctor must warn about is actionable even if the risk would arise later, to respect patient autonomy (J07:p1, J07:p2). — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **A1.6** [I5] Mr Okafor's loss is recoverable as it is the type of damage reasonably foreseeable from failure to warn of the risk. Montgomery affirms liability for losses resulting from undisclosed material risks (J06:p2), and Chester affirms causation for this form of loss even if timing differs (J07:p3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶3

## Round 1: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C1.1** [I1] *(replies to A1.1)* Although Montgomery imposes a duty to disclose material risks, it does not override the established principle in Bolam that the standard of care is judged by a responsible body of medical opinion; here, evidence from two surgeons indicates that not routinely disclosing a 2% risk is accepted medical practice, hence no breach of duty in warning arises in this case (J04:p2; F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C1.2** [I2] *(replies to A1.3)* The failure to warn Mr Okafor does not amount to breach because the medical standard under Bolam remains authoritative in assessing clinician conduct; since many responsible spinal surgeons did not routinely mention this risk in 2024, Mr Lyle's omission aligns with a body of medical opinion and thus is not negligent (J04:p2; F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C1.3** [I3] *(replies to A1.4)* The 2% risk of nerve damage is too small to require disclosure under a standard balancing the probability and gravity of harm, especially given the clinical judgement accepted by a responsible body of practitioners that such low risks need not be routinely disclosed, distinguishing Montgomery on its facts where the risk was far higher (J12:p2; J04:p2; F7). — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶2; *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C1.4** [I4] *(replies to A1.5)* Causation fails as Mr Okafor admits he might have had the surgery eventually regardless of information received; Chester v Afshar is distinguishable since that case involved a direct causal link between non-disclosure and timing of surgery, whereas here the injury would have occurred anyway, and conventional but-for causation as in Barnett applies (J08:p2; F6). — *Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington Hospital Management Committee* [1969] 1 QB 428, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C1.5** [I5] *(replies to A1.6)* Even if duty and breach are assumed, the loss claimed is too remote as it is not a direct and foreseeable consequence of the failure to warn; following The Wagon Mound principle, liability is limited to damage of a foreseeable kind, and here the permanent loss is a consequence of a recognized surgical risk with no proven causal link to the omission (J09:p3; F5). — *Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock and Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound No 1)* [1961] AC 388, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

## Round 2: Argument Agent (client)

- **A2.1** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board establishes that a doctor owes a duty to disclose to ensure the patient is aware of any material risks and reasonable alternatives before treatment, overriding the Bolam test in respect of disclosure of risks, to protect patient autonomy (J06:p2, J06:p3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3
- **A2.2** [I1] *(replies to C1.1)* The duty arises from the proximity between doctor and patient and foreseeability of harm from nondisclosure, grounded in Donoghue v Stevenson neighbour principle which applies to doctors owing duty of care to patients (J01:p3). — *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, ¶3
- **A2.3** [I2] *(replies to C1.2)* The failure to warn Mr Okafor of a roughly 2% risk and alternative treatments breaches the standard of care under Montgomery because risks material to the patient’s decision must be disclosed regardless of accepted medical practice under Bolam (J06:p2, J06:p3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3
- **A2.4** [I3] *(replies to C1.3)* The 2% risk is material as a reasonable person in Mr Okafor's position, whose career depends on fine motor control, would likely attach significance to it; the Bolam test does not govern disclosure of risks after Montgomery (J06:p3, F3). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶3
- **A2.5** [I4] *(replies to C1.4)* Causation is established following Chester v Afshar where injury from a risk a doctor must disclose is actionable even if the patient might have had the surgery later; the injury here was within the scope of the undisclosed risk, satisfying causation despite timing (J07:p1, J07:p2, F6). — *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶1; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **A2.6** [I5] *(replies to C1.5)* The loss is not too remote as it is a foreseeable kind of damage resulting from failure to warn about a material risk, consistent with Montgomery and Chester affirming recovery for such losses (J06:p2, J07:p3, F5). — *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, ¶2; *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

## Round 2: Counter-Argument Agent (other side)

- **C2.1** [I1] *(replies to A2.1)* The duty to disclose material risks and alternatives must be balanced with medical professional standards; here, evidence from two surgeons that not routinely disclosing a 2% risk aligned with accepted practice means no breach occurred (J04:p2; F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C2.2** [I2] *(replies to A2.3)* Medical standard in disclosure remains judged by a responsible body of opinion; since many spinal surgeons do not disclose this risk as routine, omission to warn Mr Okafor did not breach the standard of care (J04:p2; F7). — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C2.3** [I3] *(replies to A2.4)* A 2% risk is too low to require disclosure under standard principles balancing risk and burden; Montgomery is factually distinguishable as Mrs Montgomery faced much higher risk (J12:p2; J04:p2; F7). — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶2; *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶7
- **C2.4** [I4] *(replies to A2.5)* Causation fails since Mr Okafor admits he might have had surgery eventually, so the injury was not caused specifically by nondisclosure; Chester v Afshar is distinguishable given the stronger causal timing link in Chester (J08:p2; F6). — *Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington Hospital Management Committee* [1969] 1 QB 428, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶6
- **C2.5** [I5] *(replies to A2.6)* Loss is too remote as it was not a direct and foreseeable consequence of failing to warn but rather of an accepted surgical risk; The Wagon Mound principle limits liability to foreseeable kinds of damage, which is not shown here for omission to warn (J09:p3; F5). — *Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock and Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound No 1)* [1961] AC 388, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
