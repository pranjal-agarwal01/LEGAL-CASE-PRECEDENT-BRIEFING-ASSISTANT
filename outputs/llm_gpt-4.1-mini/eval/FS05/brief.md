# Brief: Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd v SwiftHaul Couriers Ltd

**Case** FS05 · **Acting for** Claimant · **Prepared** 15 November 2026 · **Mode** llm:foundry

> Academic demonstration only. This is not legal advice. It was produced by a multi-agent prototype from a synthetic fact sheet and a small teaching corpus of paraphrased judgment summaries; verify any authority against the official law report.

## Summary

- Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd contracted SwiftHaul Couriers Ltd for guaranteed next-day delivery of a vital electronic component, crucial for their business operation (F2). — Fact sheet ¶2
- SwiftHaul delayed delivery by eleven days due to misrouting, breaching the promised delivery standard and causing significant lost profits and a special lost contract (F4,F5). — Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶5

## Issues

1. **I1.** Did SwiftHaul owe a duty of care in the contractual performance to Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd?
2. **I2.** Was the standard of care in delivery breached by SwiftHaul's error?
3. **I3.** Are Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd's lost profits and lost wedding contract recoverable as damages?
4. **I4.** Is clause 9 of SwiftHaul's terms excluding liability for loss of profits enforceable against Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd?
5. **I5.** Could SwiftHaul reasonably contemplate the loss of profits and the lost wedding contract at the time of contracting with Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd?

## Key facts

- The bakery's only industrial oven's controller board failed and required repair to be delivered to Birmingham. — Fact sheet ¶1
- The bakery booked SwiftHaul's guaranteed next-day delivery online describing the parcel as 'electronic component' and did not disclose inability to trade without the part. — Fact sheet ¶2
- The bakery accepted SwiftHaul's terms including an exclusion clause limiting liability for loss of profits arising from delay. — Fact sheet ¶3
- SwiftHaul mistakenly sent the parcel to the wrong depot resulting in an eleven-day delay in delivery. — Fact sheet ¶4
- The bakery lost ordinary trade profits of about £18,000 and a £40,000 one-off wedding contract due to inability to bake for twelve days. — Fact sheet ¶5
- SwiftHaul contends it did not foresee the bakery's inability to trade or the exceptional nature of the wedding contract and relies on the exclusion clause to exclude these losses. — Fact sheet ¶6
- The bakery seeks damages for breach of contract covering lost profits and lost contract due to delayed delivery. — Fact sheet ¶7

## I1. Did SwiftHaul owe a duty of care in the contractual performance to Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4 | Ordinary negligence principles apply to positive acts creating foreseeable risk of physical injury, establishing a duty of care. | *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605 | No duty of care beyond contract without sufficient proximity; commercial delivery services do not create such special relationships for economic loss claims. | *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- SwiftHaul owed a duty of care under ordinary negligence principles because its positive act of delivering the parcel created a reasonably foreseeable risk of harm if mishandled, similar to police liability in Robinson. — *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2

**Argument for the other side**

- The relationship was purely contractual with no special proximity, so no separate duty of care in negligence should be imposed beyond contractual terms, as explained in Caparo. — *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶3

**Assessment**

- Given the commercial context and absence of physical harm risk, SwiftHaul's duty is primarily contractual; however, the bakery's evidence of reliance on timely delivery supports application of ordinary negligence principles to the delivery's positive act, supporting a duty of care. — *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2; *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2

## I2. Was the standard of care in delivery breached by SwiftHaul's error?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691 | Standard of care is objective; a failure to meet promised service levels constitutes breach regardless of defendant's subjective abilities. | *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶2 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850 | Not all foreseeable risks amount to breach; low-probability errors may be excused if precautions were reasonable and practicable. | *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- SwiftHaul breached the objective standard of care by failing to meet the guaranteed next-day delivery, causing an eleven-day delay which no reasonable courier would tolerate. — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶2

**Argument for the other side**

- A single isolated routing error may not amount to breach as courts weigh low probability and practicability of prevention; thus SwiftHaul’s actions may have been reasonable under Bolton v Stone. — *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, ¶3

**Assessment**

- The delay was significant and undermined the guaranteed next-day service; objectively this constitutes breach of standard of care despite any isolated nature of the error. — *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶2

## I3. Are Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd's lost profits and lost wedding contract recoverable as damages?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528 | Losses naturally arising from breach, and within defendants’ knowledge at contracting, are recoverable; ordinary lost profits are recoverable but exceptional unknown contracts are not. | *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341 | Losses are recoverable only if naturally arising or within parties’ contemplation; unknown special circumstances prevent recovery beyond ordinary losses. | *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2 |

**Argument for the client**

- The bakery's ordinary lost profits arose naturally from the breach since SwiftHaul knew the parcel was essential for immediate use; thus these are recoverable as in Victoria Laundry. — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5

**Argument for the other side**

- The bakery did not communicate special circumstances like the wedding contract, so those losses are not recoverable and ordinary losses are limited to natural consequences of delay. — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5

**Assessment**

- Ordinary lost profits are likely recoverable due to SwiftHaul's knowledge of the delivery's importance, but the exceptional lost wedding contract remains unrecoverable absent specific communication. — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5

## I4. Is clause 9 of SwiftHaul's terms excluding liability for loss of profits enforceable against Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Interfoto Picture Library Ltd v Stiletto Visual Programmes Ltd* [1989] QB 433 | Onerous and unusual exclusion clauses must be fairly and reasonably brought to the other party's attention to be incorporated. | *Interfoto Picture Library Ltd v Stiletto Visual Programmes Ltd* [1989] QB 433, ¶2 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394 | A party who signs or accepts terms is bound by them regardless of reading, absent fraud or misrepresentation. | *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2 |
| *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827 | Clearly worded exclusion clauses in commercial contracts between equal parties are generally upheld. | *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- Clause 9 is onerous and unusual and was not fairly and reasonably brought to the bakery’s attention in the online booking process, so it is unenforceable under Interfoto. — *Interfoto Picture Library Ltd v Stiletto Visual Programmes Ltd* [1989] QB 433, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

**Argument for the other side**

- The bakery accepted the terms by ticking the box, binding it to clause 9 despite lack of highlighted notice, per L'Estrange and Photo Production principles. — Fact sheet ¶3; *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394, ¶2; *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, ¶3

**Assessment**

- Given the clause's onerous nature and the context of online booking, there is strong argument clause 9 was insufficiently highlighted and thus unenforceable against the bakery. — *Interfoto Picture Library Ltd v Stiletto Visual Programmes Ltd* [1989] QB 433, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3

## I5. Could SwiftHaul reasonably contemplate the loss of profits and the lost wedding contract at the time of contracting with Kaur Artisan Bakery Ltd?

**Supporting precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528 | Losses are recoverable if within defendants' contemplation at contracting, judged by their knowledge including imputed knowledge of ordinary course and actual knowledge of special circumstances. | *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶3 |

**Opposing precedents**

| Case | Proposition | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341 | Defendants must reasonably foresee losses at contracting; unknown special losses are not within contemplation. | *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶3 |

**Argument for the client**

- SwiftHaul could reasonably foresee the bakery’s ordinary lost profits due to the next-day guarantee and the nature of the parcel, fulfilling the knowledge requirement for recoverability. — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶2; Fact sheet ¶5

**Argument for the other side**

- SwiftHaul could not have reasonably foreseen the exceptional lost wedding contract as the bakery failed to notify them of special circumstances, limiting recoverability to ordinary losses only. — *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

**Assessment**

- Losses from ordinary profits are likely within SwiftHaul’s reasonable contemplation, but the exceptional lost wedding contract was unforeseeable and thus non-recoverable. — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶3; *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5

## Conclusion

- SwiftHaul owed a duty of care in the delivery and breached the objective standard by an eleven-day delay in guaranteed next-day service, causing foreseeable loss to the bakery. — *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, ¶2; *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶4; Fact sheet ¶2
- The bakery's ordinary lost profits are recoverable damages as they naturally arose from the breach and were within SwiftHaul’s reasonable contemplation, but the special lost wedding contract is not recoverable due to lack of notice. — *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶2; *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528, ¶3; *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶2; *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341, ¶3; Fact sheet ¶5
- Clause 9 excluding liability for loss of profits is likely unenforceable against the bakery because it was an onerous term not fairly and reasonably brought to their attention in the online booking process. — *Interfoto Picture Library Ltd v Stiletto Visual Programmes Ltd* [1989] QB 433, ¶2; Fact sheet ¶3
